## The map is not the territory

Alfred Korzybski put it simply: **the map is not the territory; the word is not the thing.** The label "idiot" is a map. The person is the territory: a history, a tired evening, a bad day, strengths you cannot see.

A label takes one reaction at one moment and presents it as the whole person. "You are stupid" sounds like a fact, but it is a judgement. Korzybski called this the "*is* of identity": someone *does* something, and we say they *are* it.

## Pause

An insult is almost always a signal reaction: something stings, we strike back. General semantics suggests a **delayed reaction**: read, breathe, then answer. A few seconds turn a reflex into a choice.

## Three questions before sending

- Am I talking about an action or about a person?
- Is this a fact or my judgement?
- What do I want: a solution, or for the other person to feel bad?

## The same thing, said precisely

- "You're an idiot" → "I see a mistake in this decision: …"
- "You're all morons" → "It makes me angry that …"
- "Shut up" → "Let's get back to the topic."

A precise sentence is stronger: people can answer it on the merits, while a label only invites another label.

## Alfred Korzybski (1879–1950)

An engineer from Poland who fought in the First World War. Having seen that war, he asked why human technology advances so fast while human relations advance so slowly. The answers became *Manhood of Humanity* (1921), on *time-binding*, the human ability to pass experience on to the next generations, and *Science and Sanity* (1933), the basis of general semantics. In 1938 he founded the Institute of General Semantics.

## Jacque Fresco (1916–2017)

Industrial designer and futurist, creator of The Venus Project. He proposed applying the scientific method to social problems and spoke a lot about language: imprecise words lead to imprecise decisions. More in the document about SocioCyberneering Inc.
